PERSPECTIVE / EDITORIAL DRAFT

What we fear
when we fear AI.

Income. Competence. Control.
These are different problems. They deserve different questions.

Fear AIDraft · November 2026About 4 minutes

IN THIS PIECE

01 — Work02 — Trust03 — Agency04 — Our method

“Fear AI” sounds like an instruction. We prefer to treat it as a question. What do we fear, exactly—and what would count as evidence?

Someone worried about losing a livelihood is asking a different question from someone unsettled by an artificial companion. A person checking an answer is doing something different from an organization deciding who gets to make a decision. An argument about “AI” can slide between all of these without anyone noticing.

Before choosing a side, it helps to choose a question.

01 / The work after the task

A job is a bundle of responsibilities, relationships and tasks. Making one task easier can change that bundle. It does not tell us, by itself, how many people an employer will hire, what those people will earn, or who gets the benefit of the saved time.

Those are questions about choices as well as capabilities. Does a team do more work? Do its members work fewer hours? Does the work become more demanding? Does someone lose the chance to learn by doing?

We want to keep the distance between a model demonstration and an employment claim visible. An impressive output is a reason to investigate what changes next. It is not an answer to every question about work.

02 / The answer and the authority

A convincing voice can make a claim feel settled before we have examined it. The useful question is not whether the machine sounds confident. It is whether a person can trace the answer, test it and correct it.

A useful assistant should leave room for an independent judgment.

That means more than attaching a link. A source needs to support the particular claim being made. A forecast needs its assumptions. A number needs a denominator. A quotation needs its context. And someone needs the time and authority to say that an answer is insufficient.

We can find a tool useful and still question the institution deploying it. We can enjoy an interaction and still ask what kind of relationship it creates. Use and doubt can coexist.

03 / Who gets to decide?

Fear becomes more concrete when people cannot inspect or influence a change that affects them. Who selects the tool? Who defines a good result? Who handles an error? Who can refuse—and at what cost?

These questions remain human even when a task becomes automated. A system can make a recommendation; an institution chooses how much authority to give it. Calling a decision technical does not remove the choices behind it.

The same discipline applies to the physical buildout. Electricity, water, land and infrastructure deserve their own evidence. We keep that inquiry in a separate data-center section, where capacity and consumption can be examined on their own terms.

04 / How we assess claims

Fear AI is a place for critical writing, visual commentary and experiments you can inspect. We start with the question, separate observation from interpretation, and follow a claim back to its source.

We do not need a final verdict on artificial intelligence to ask a useful question about a specific system. We need enough curiosity to look closely, and enough independence to change our minds.
